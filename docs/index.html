Before anything else

Five things to know

This page is a small experiment that runs on you, so here is what it does before it does it.

  1. You will walk the same trip across the DTU Lyngby campus fourteen times, one day at a time, choosing between three real footpath routes drawn from OpenStreetMap.
  2. Each route has a true walking time you are not shown. You learn it only by walking it, and only the route you took reports back, which is the situation a person is actually in.
  3. On one of the days a path closes without warning, and the route you have settled into stops being available.
  4. At the end, the page fits two numbers to your fourteen choices: how fast you update a belief after one walk, and how decisively you act on the belief you hold.
  5. Then it tells you how much those two numbers are worth, by having already run the same fit on hundreds of simulated people whose numbers were known. That part is the reason the page exists.
Nothing you do here leaves your browser

There is no server, no upload, no analytics and no storage. The fit runs in your own machine and disappears when you close the tab. That is also why the page cannot pool across visitors, which is a real limitation and not a virtue.

Terms, in the order they appear
route One of three ways across campus, each a real chain of footpaths.
belief What you currently think a route costs in minutes. It starts at what the map implies and moves when you walk.
learning rate How much of the difference between what you expected and what you got is absorbed after one walk. Zero means you never update; one means you replace your belief with the last walk.
decisiveness How strongly a small difference in belief turns into always picking the better route. Low means you wander; high means you commit.
recovery Feeding the fitter data from an agent whose numbers are known, to see whether it gets them back. If it cannot, the number it gives you means little.
stable preference The assumption that a person has a fixed ranking of routes that does not move with experience. It is the assumption this project exists to question.
Their words

The sentence this page is built on

The advertisement for the Brains in Motion scholarship states the gap plainly, and the whole of this page is one small attempt at the thing it asks for.

Navigating modern cities is a complex and cognitively demanding task. People continuously learn, adapt and update their behaviours [...] Yet, current models of navigation and route choice often assume stable preferences and near-perfect knowledge of the environment. As a result, they provide limited insights into how people learn to move through cities over time. DTU Management, PhD scholarship 7860, Human Navigation, Artificial Intelligence and Neuroscience, read 24 August 2026

So this experiment withholds knowledge of the environment on purpose, forces preferences to move, and then measures whether the moving can be detected at all from the amount of data a real study would collect. Act four fits the stable-preference model to your own choices and shows what it concludes about you, which is the concrete version of the complaint above.

Scope, stated plainly. This is a route-choice experiment with fourteen trials and one participant. It is not a study of wayfinding in the sense of finding your way when you are lost, it collects no eye movements and no physiological signal, and nothing here has been through an ethics committee, which is why it takes no data off your machine.

Act one

Fourteen days across campus

Why this matters Every number later on the page comes from what you do in this box. It takes about two minutes.

You are at the start and you need to get to the goal. Three routes, drawn from the real footpath network. Pick one for today. You will be told how long it took, and only for the route you chose.

Click a route on the map, or the button under it. The clock starts when the day begins, because how long you take to decide is part of what is measured.

Press begin.
Act two

What those fourteen choices say about you

Why this matters This is the model the project is about: beliefs that move with experience rather than preferences fixed in advance.

The fit searches a grid of learning rates and decisiveness values for the pair that makes your actual sequence of choices most likely. The chart underneath shows what your beliefs did, day by day, under the best-fitting pair.

Finish act one and this fills in.

Act three

What fourteen days can actually carry

Why this matters Two numbers were just printed next to your name. This act is about whether they mean anything, and it was run before you arrived.

Simulated people with known learning rates and known decisiveness walked exactly this experiment, and exactly this fitter was asked to recover their numbers. The answer decides how much weight your own fit can bear.

Read the curve as the answer to "how many days would a real study need". The marked point is where this page sits.

Act four

What a stable-preference model concludes about you

Why this matters This is the advertisement's complaint, made into a number on your own data.

The same fit is run again with learning switched off, so beliefs never move from what the map implied. If that version explains your choices about as well as the learning one, then fourteen days of one person cannot tell the two apart, whatever is true of the person.

Finish act one and this fills in.

How it was built

Method, limits and sources

This page checking its own fitter

The recovery study was run in Python and the live fit runs in JavaScript, so the two could disagree without anyone noticing. The Python run therefore shipped one worked example with its answer, and the browser refits it on load.

Running.

The map

The model that is fitted

Each route carries a believed time, starting at its length divided by a plain walking speed of 1.35 metres per second, which is what a map alone would let you guess. After a walk, the belief for the route you took moves toward what you observed by the learning rate, and no other belief moves, because you learned nothing about routes you did not take. A choice is drawn from a softmax over the negative believed times, with decisiveness as its scale. Both numbers are found by searching a grid for the maximum likelihood of your actual choices. The ranges quoted beside them are every grid value within two log-likelihood units of the best, which is the usual rule of thumb for a value that is not meaningfully worse.

Response time is recorded but is not fitted. Combining choice with response time properly needs a sequential sampling model, which is the kind of model Rico Krueger's own work builds, and fourteen trials from one person cannot support one. Act three is the evidence for that claim rather than an excuse for it: if fourteen trials cannot pin two parameters, they will not carry five.

Limits, stated before you find them

One participant, one trip, one campus. Nothing here generalises to a population, and the page cannot pool across visitors because it deliberately keeps your data on your machine.

The true walking times are not measured in the field. They come from real path lengths and real path types, with a hidden per-segment friction drawn once from a seeded distribution to stand for what a map cannot show. The learning problem is therefore real and the minutes are plausible, but they are not observations of anybody walking.

Choosing between three routes laid out on a map is not the same act as finding your way through a city while moving. It is the part of the problem that fits in two minutes in a browser, and the distance between the two is the reason this is a work sample and not a finding.
